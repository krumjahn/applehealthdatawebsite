What is sleep debt?

Sleep debt (also called a sleep deficit) is the running difference between the sleep your body needs and the sleep it actually gets. If you need 8 hours a night but average 6.5, you accrue 1.5 hours of debt every day. Over a five-day work week that is 7.5 hours — nearly a full night gone. Unlike a bank account, this debt is invisible: you adapt to feeling tired, so your judgment of how impaired you are gets worse at exactly the moment the debt is largest.

Acute vs. chronic sleep debt

Acute debt is a short burst — a few bad nights before a deadline or a trip. It is uncomfortable but recoverable within a few days of normal sleep. Chronic debt builds when small nightly shortfalls repeat for weeks or months. Chronic debt is the one that matters for long-term health: sustained deficits of even 30–60 minutes a night are associated in the research literature with slower reaction times, reduced insulin sensitivity (poorer glucose control), and lower mood and irritability. This calculator measures a rolling 7-night window, which is the sweet spot for catching a slide into chronic debt before it compounds.

How the debt is calculated

For each night we take your sleep need minus the hours you actually slept. Nights below your need add to the debt. Nights above your need give back credit — but only up to +1 hour per night. That cap is deliberate and reflects the biology: you cannot meaningfully "bank" extra sleep in advance, and even for recovery, sleeping far past your need produces diminishing returns and mostly lighter sleep. So one 11-hour lie-in after a 40-hour work week barely dents the deficit. The tool sums the seven nightly figures into one weekly sleep-debt number, then places it in a severity band.

Why Apple Watch users underestimate their debt

Apple Health can report two different things: Time in Bed and Asleep. Time in Bed counts from the moment you lie down to the moment you get up, including the time it takes to fall asleep and every mid-night awakening. Asleep is the estimate of actual sleep. The gap between them — your sleep latency plus awakenings — is often 20–40 minutes and can be much more. If you plug Time in Bed into any sleep-debt tool, your debt will look smaller than it really is. Always use the Asleep figure. This is the single most common reason people are surprised to find they are more sleep-deprived than their watch suggested.

How to pay it back (without wrecking your rhythm)

The instinct is to sleep 12–14 hours on Saturday. Don't. Marathon weekend sleep shifts your body clock later, so Sunday night you can't fall asleep, and Monday you start the new week already behind — a pattern researchers call "social jet lag." The evidence-based approach is gradual: add about one extra hour to your normal sleep for the next 1–2 weeks by going to bed a little earlier, and take short naps of 30 minutes or less early in the afternoon if you need them. Frequently asked questions

How is sleep debt calculated?

Sleep debt is the sum of the shortfall between your nightly sleep need and how much you actually slept, across the last 7 nights. If you need 8 hours and sleep 6, that night adds 2 hours of debt. Nights where you sleep more than your need offset debt only slightly, because you cannot fully bank extra sleep — this tool caps each night's surplus credit at +1 hour.

How much sleep debt do I have?

Enter your sleep need (default 8 hours) and how many hours you slept each of the past seven nights. The calculator sums each night's deficit, caps surplus nights at +1 hour of credit, and shows your total weekly sleep debt with a severity band from minimal to severe.

Can I catch up on sleep on the weekend?

Only partially. Sleeping 12–14 hours on a Saturday does not erase a week of deficit and disrupts your body clock, making Sunday night worse. Recovery is best spread over one to two weeks by adding about an hour extra per night plus short naps of 30 minutes or less. This tool builds that realistic plan for you.

Why does Apple Watch overestimate my sleep?

Apple Health may record time in bed rather than time actually asleep. Time in bed includes falling-asleep minutes and awakenings, so it can read 20–40 minutes higher than true sleep. Use the "Asleep" total from Apple Health, not "Time in Bed", or your sleep debt will look smaller than it really is.

Is this sleep debt calculator private?

Yes. Everything runs entirely in your browser. Your sleep numbers are never uploaded, stored, or sent anywhere. You can even use the tool offline once the page has loaded.

What is a healthy amount of sleep debt?

A small amount of fluctuating debt is normal. Under about 2 hours across a week is considered minimal. Chronic debt above 5 hours per week, sustained over months, is linked to slower reaction time, impaired glucose control, and lower mood, so it is worth actively recovering from.
